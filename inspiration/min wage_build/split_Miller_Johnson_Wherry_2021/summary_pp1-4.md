# Miller, Johnson & Wherry (2021) — Summary, pp. 1-4

## What these pages argue
Title page, abstract, Introduction (Sec. I), and start of Background (Sec. II). The paper links large-scale federal survey data (ACS) to administrative death and Medicaid-enrollment records to estimate the effect of ACA Medicaid expansions on mortality among near-elderly (55-64) low-income adults. The core innovation is the **individual-level linkage** of survey characteristics (income, citizenship, program participation) to objective administrative mortality — overcoming the two prior literatures' weaknesses: (1) survey studies with self-reported health, (2) aggregate death-certificate studies that lack individual eligibility info and are underpowered / may overstate magnitudes (Black et al. 2019).

## Key concepts
- Design: compare mortality changes in expansion vs. non-expansion states for adults likely to benefit.
- Motivation: low-income older adults have 1.7% annual mortality vs. 0.4% for higher-income — 4x higher; elevated death risk from diabetes (+787%), cardiovascular disease (+552%), respiratory disease (+813%). Chetty et al. (2016): ~15-year (men) / 10-year (women) life-expectancy gap by income.
- Policy: 2014 ACA expanded Medicaid to all adults <138% FPL; 2012 Supreme Court made it optional; 29 states + DC expanded in 2014, 7 more later. ~13.6 million adults gained Medicaid (vs. 19M for Medicare in 1965).

## Main numbers / results (headline, stated in abstract & intro)
- **0.132 pp decline in annual mortality**, a **9.4% reduction** over the sample mean (main DD estimate).
- Year 1: **0.089 pp decline (6.4% reduction)**; grows over time; **Year 4: 0.208 pp reduction**.
- Effect driven by disease-related (internal) deaths; no decline in external causes (e.g., car accidents).
- Policy counterfactual: ~**15,600 deaths could have been averted** if all states expanded.

### DiD ELEMENTS
1. **BITE / FIRST STAGE**: Framed but not yet quantified here. Notes population-level coverage rose "as little as 1 percentage point among all nonelderly adults" (Black et al. 2019) — motivating their sharper, individual-targeted sample. ~13.6M gained Medicaid nationally. Affected group defined by low SES.
2. **FALSIFICATIONS**: Previewed — placebo tests on (a) those 65+ in 2014 (no new eligibility), (b) higher-income families (less affected), (c) pre-ACA period. Report no relative change in elderly/pre-ACA; small significant coverage gains + small mortality declines among higher-income (consistent with causal Medicaid effect).
3. **EVENT STUDIES**: Previewed — pre-expansion trends similar; divergence begins in first policy year; effect grows over time.
4. **MAIN RESULTS**: 0.132 pp / 9.4% mortality reduction; year1 0.089 pp; year4 0.208 pp; 15,600 avertable deaths.
5. **MECHANISM**: Reduction concentrated in disease-related / healthcare-amenable / internal causes; no effect on external causes.

### DATA DETAILS
- **ACS** 2008-2013 (restricted-use), ~4M respondents/year; identifies low-income target population.
- **Census Numident file** (SSA-derived date of death for SSN holders) — mortality through 2017.
- **CMS Medicaid enrollment** administrative records.
- **MDAC (Mortality Disparities in American Communities)**: 2008 ACS linked to death-certificate cause-of-death via National Death Index (2008-2015).
- Restricted-access: performed at FSRDC, Project 1743; Census DRB disclosure review (CBDRB numbers listed).
