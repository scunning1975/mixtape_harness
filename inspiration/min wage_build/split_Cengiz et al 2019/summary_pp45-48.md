# Cengiz, Dube, Lindner & Zipperer (2019), pp. 45–48 (journal pp. 1449–1452)

## What these pages argue
These pages diagnose why the classic **TWFE-logMW** specification produces spurious aggregate disemployment, then deliver the paper's **Discussion/conclusion**. The distributional lens exposes that the negative aggregate elasticity in TWFE-logMW comes almost entirely from **upper-tail wage bins (>$15/hour)** — implausible for a minimum wage binding near $8–$9. The authors trace this to pre-event confounding from the 1980s expansion and 1990–91 recession, and conclude their event-study design avoids this.

## Key concepts / results
- The aggregate negative elasticity (e.g., **−0.137**) accrues almost entirely in bins **above $15/hour**, which cannot plausibly be caused by a low minimum wage — evidence of confounding.
- The negative changes appear only for the **Card-Krueger low-probability group** (workers who should be unaffected); high- and medium-probability groups show no disemployment (Online Appendix Table G.2).
- Losses are driven by the **1980s expansion and 1990–91 recession**, before most minimum wage variation. Restricting to **1993–2016** — where **86% (118/138)** of events occur — makes TWFE-logMW and the event study agree, both showing small effects and no upper-tail changes (Online Appendix Figure G.5).
- Employment-to-population in high- vs. low-minimum-wage states tracked closely after the early 1990s but had **diverged during the 1980s**, creating a spurious correlation that only the long-panel TWFE-logMW picks up. The event study, confined to the event window, shows no pre-trends.
- The 1980s/1990–91 shocks were more pronounced in states that later became Democratic-leaning; predictive power vanishes after controlling for 2000s partisan voting index (instrumented with 1988 PVI).
- This reconciles literature disputes (e.g., sensitivity of teen employment estimates to trend controls; Neumark-Salas-Wascher 2014, Allegretto et al. 2017): post-1992, teen elasticities are small, no more negative than **−0.03**, and robust to trend controls.

## Discussion (Section V) — main takeaways
- Employment effects inferred from the change in the **frequency distribution of wages**, using **138 prominent minimum wage increases**.
- Excess jobs at/just above the new minimum closely match missing jobs just below; **no employment change at or above $4 over the minimum**. Same pattern for low-skilled workers implies **labor-labor substitution is not a factor**.
- Minimum wages studied (**37%–59% of median**) have not reached the point where job losses become sizable; higher minimums and tradeable-sector exposure remain open concerns.
- Distributional tracking serves as a **falsification/model-selection tool** and a way to infer low-wage labor market structure.

### DATA DETAILS
- Restricted-period robustness uses **1993–2016** (86% = 118/138 of events) and a subsample of **39 states** with no state minimum wage increases until the late 1990s.
- **Replication archive:** Data and code are in **Cengiz et al. (2019), Harvard Dataverse, doi:10.7910/DVN/TJCTC7**. An Online Appendix is available at QJE.
