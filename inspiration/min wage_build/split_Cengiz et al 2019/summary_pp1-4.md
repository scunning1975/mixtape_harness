# Cengiz, Dube, Lindner & Zipperer (2019) — Summary pp. 1–4 (QJE pp. 1405–1408)

**What these pages argue.** Title page, abstract, and opening Introduction. The paper estimates the effect of minimum wages on low-wage jobs using a difference-in-differences (event study) design on 138 prominent state-level minimum wage changes, 1979–2016. The innovation: estimate the effect on the *entire frequency distribution of wages by wage bin*, then focus on the bottom, comparing "excess jobs" at/above the new minimum with "missing jobs" below it to infer net employment.

**Key concepts.** Bunching logic — a hike destroys/reshuffles below-floor jobs (missing jobs) and creates a spike of excess jobs at/slightly above; net effect = excess − missing; effects fade above a threshold. Upper-tail employment changes serve as a falsification test. Localized focus filters noise. Motivated by the 2014 CBO report, which extrapolated from teen elasticities.

**Main results/numbers.**
- Low-wage jobs essentially unchanged over 5 years post-increase.
- Employment of affected workers rose an insignificant **2.8% (s.e. 2.9%)**.
- Average wages of affected workers rose **~6.8% (s.e. 1.0%)** — the "bite".
- Own-wage employment elasticity = **0.41 (s.e. 0.43)**; rules out elasticities below **−0.45** at 95%.
- Wage spillovers extend to **$3 above** the minimum, ~**40%** of total wage increase; accrue to incumbents, not new entrants.
- Robust to time-varying heterogeneity controls; parallel pre-trends; immediate, durable effects.

**Figures/tables.** None (title/abstract/intro text only).

### DATA DETAILS
- Hourly wage data from the **Current Population Survey (CPS)**, **1979–2016**.
- **138 prominent state-level minimum wage changes**, 1979–2016, United States.
- DiD/event study; employment by **dollar wage bin** relative to new minimum, **3 years pre to 5 years post**.
- Administrative hourly-wage data shared by **Minnesota Dept. of Employment and Economic Development, Oregon Employment Department, Washington State Employment Security Department**.
- Minimum-wage-worker share of production ≈ **2%** in US.
