# Summary: Cengiz, Dube, Lindner & Zipperer (2019), pp. 1433–1436

## What these pages argue
These pages finish the demographic-heterogeneity analysis (by exposure-probability group) and begin the industry-sector analysis (Section III.A.2). Two conclusions: (1) the most precise own-wage employment elasticity in the paper comes from combining Card-Krueger demographic profiling with the low-wage-jobs approach, and it rejects large negative elasticities; (2) there is little heterogeneity in employment effects by skill, so the null overall effect does not hide a low-skill-for-high-skill substitution. The sector analysis then shows the minimum wage bites very differently across industries.

## Key concepts / equations
- Exposure groups: **high-probability** = top 10% of predicted exposure; **low-probability** = bottom 50%; **middle** = the rest.
- Same estimator (equation (1)) as baseline; elasticity formulae unchanged.
- **Tradeable / nontradeable / construction** sectors defined following Mian & Sufi (2014) — tradeable = imports+exports > $10,000/worker or > $500M total; nontradeable = subset of restaurant/retail; construction = construction/real-estate/land-development.

## Main results / numbers
- Exposure-group bite (Δb): high −9.4%, middle −2.0%, low −0.4%; elasticities similar across groups.
- **Most precise elasticity** (Table II col 6, high-probability group): CI rejects values below −0.251 and above 0.663.
- High-probability group captures only ~36% of all minimum-wage workers — motivating the low-wage-jobs approach for full coverage.
- Sector bite (Δb): restaurants highly binding at −10.1%; nontradeable −6.6%; tradeable −1.6%; manufacturing −1.7%; construction not binding (−0.3%, insignificant).
- Employment: tradeable −11.1% (s.e. 13.6%) and manufacturing −10.1% (s.e. 14.5%), both negative but insignificant; own-wage elasticities −1.910 (tradeable) and −1.385 (manufacturing), imprecise.
- Nontradeable own-wage elasticity positive **0.387** (s.e. 0.597); no disemployment in nontradeable, restaurant, retail (where most MW workers are). Harasztosi & Lindner (Hungary) find matching sectoral patterns.

### DATA DETAILS
- **Table II:** 138 events; 847,314 obs (846,729 col 5) quarter-bin cells; worker counts per group as listed pp.1433.
- **Table III sample:** restricted to **1992–2016** (consistent industry classification); **118 events**; **554,931 observations** (quarter-bin cells) in every column.
- **Table III workers per sector:** overall 2,652,792; tradeable 358,086; nontradeable 384,498; construction 274,812; other 1,504,643; restaurants 156,634; retail 315,397; manufacturing 349,749.
- **Sector employment shares** in sample: tradeable 13%, nontradeable 14%, construction 10%.
- **Industry classification:** Mian & Sufi (2014) four-digit NAICS list + census industry crosswalks applied to CPS 1992–2016 (details in Online Appendix D/E).
- **Card-Krueger predictors:** three-way interactions of nonwhite×gender×teen and nonwhite×gender×age20-25; less-than-HS indicator; highest-grade-completed; third-order polynomial in experience; Hispanic indicator; education×experience×gender interactions. Cengiz (2018) validates against ML methods.
- Regressions weighted by state-quarter aggregated population; SEs clustered by state.
