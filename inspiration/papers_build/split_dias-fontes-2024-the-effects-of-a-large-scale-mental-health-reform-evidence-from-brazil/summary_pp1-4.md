# Dias & Fontes (2024), "The Effects of a Large-Scale Mental Health Reform: Evidence from Brazil" — pp. 1-4 (AEJ: Economic Policy 16(3): 257-289)

## What these pages argue
Abstract, intro, literature, and start of institutional background. The paper studies Brazil's 2002 Psychiatric Reform, which reorganized public mental-health provision by introducing **CAPS** (Centros de Atenção Psicossocial / Psychosocial Care Centers) as a **community-based substitute for inpatient (psychiatric hospital) care**. Using the staggered rollout of CAPS across municipalities in a DiD design, the headline findings are: CAPS (1) increased outpatient mental-health care production and the density of mental-health professionals; (2) reduced psychiatric hospitalizations (concentrated in long-stay admissions and schizophrenia patients); (3) reduced hospitalization spending but NOT enough to offset program cost (net financial loss); (4) did NOT reduce mental-health mortality; and (5) INCREASED homicides/violent crime — consistent with the Penrose hypothesis (inpatient care incapacitates violence-prone patients). JEL H51, I12, I18, O15.

### BITE / FIRST STAGE
This is the substitution mechanism the whole paper rests on. Key institutional facts for building a bite measure:
- **What a CAPS is:** the primary/gateway source of public mental-health care, staffed by a multidisciplinary team (psychiatrists, psychologists, occupational therapists, social workers). Provides psychosocial/outpatient procedures: talk therapy, occupational therapy, medical consultations, medications. Explicitly designed to substitute for psychiatric inpatient beds.
- **How a municipality "gets" one (rollout timing):** municipality must APPLY to the federal government. No formal prioritization criteria. Applications almost never rejected (only rejection reason = below population threshold). Type applied for depends on population thresholds: **CAPS I (15,000-75,000), CAPS II (75,000-150,000), CAPS III (>150,000)**; plus special types CAPS i (children) and CAPS AD (drug/alcohol). Population criteria "important but not strictly binding."
- **First-stage outcomes named (measured later):** density of mental-health professionals, density of psychiatric beds, outpatient mental-health visits/production, antipsychotic drugs dispensed, hospital admissions (mental illness), spending. The paper "first documents" immediate significant increases in professional density and outpatient visits, plus more drugs dispensed = the treatment/bite.
- **Untreated margin:** psychiatric beds had ALREADY been declining since the 1980s independent of CAPS (noted p.4/next chunk) — flagged as a complication for reading bed reductions as CAPS-caused.
- **Cost of policy:** ~1.3 billion 2019 BRL (~US$280M) over 2002-2014; federal support per CAPS: 800,000-1,000,000 BRL construction + 30,000-100,000 BRL/month maintenance.

### DiD DESIGN
- Staggered rollout of CAPS across municipalities; treatment = municipality receives its first CAPS.
- Estimator: **de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfoeuille (2020)**, robust to heterogeneous treatment effects across cohorts/time (explicitly contrasted with biased TWFE per Goodman-Bacon, Sun-Abraham).
- Unit: **municipality** (minimum comparable areas).
- Outcomes studied: professional/bed density, outpatient production, spending, hospital admissions and DEATHS by cause (mental/behavioral disorders, suicide, alcohol, overdose), and HOMICIDES. Yes — studies homicides, suicides, hospitalizations, and mortality.

### DATA DETAILS
- Primary source: **Brazilian Ministry of Health / DATASUS** administrative records. CAPS opening dates/types from Ministry of Health (2019). Period covered 2002-2019 for CAPS openings; variation exploited from 2003 on. 80% of CAPS-municipalities have CAPS I. Only 13 closures ignored.
- Patient-mix note (Appendix Table A1): nationally 76% of diagnosed mentally ill have depression, 6% schizophrenia; but in CAPS only 12% depression, 28% schizophrenia (CAPS treat medium/high severity).
